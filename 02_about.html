// MANIFESTO //

WHILE WE WERE DRAWING

Architecture bears responsibility for physical space. It conceives and shapes the spaces in which life unfolds. In doing so, it designs the conditions of our lives. Yet our lives increasingly unfold in digital space as well. The digital changes how we think, how we act and, ultimately, our way of being. This digital layer is now deeply woven into our existence and into physical space.

Architecture therefore needs more courage to invite the digital world into the design process. Digital tools already change how we plan and build, but the digital is not merely a tool. Lines in CAD and digital volumes can replace paper and polystyrene, but they have little to say about the connectivity, speed and dislocation of the global digital apparatus. In my master’s thesis, I tried to open the design process to these questions. I first designed architectural structures, “Urban Creatures”, which then travelled through the digital world as images. An automated loop connected comments from social media with AI models and generated new images every day. The structures evolved autonomously, shaped by intuitive thoughts from different people around the world.

The starting point for this experiment was the realisation that the digital does not wait for our invitation. This becomes clear when technology companies attempt to shape physical space themselves. Sidewalk Labs, then part of Alphabet alongside Google, planned an entire urban district in Toronto. The ambition was to create a district “built from the internet up”—a living environment shaped by the company’s technological possibilities and ideas. The project faced public concerns about privacy. In 2020, Sidewalk Labs withdrew, citing economic uncertainty. For me, this attempt reveals a shift in the power to shape space: a technology corporation transfers its understanding of human behaviour from the digital into the physical realm. I began exploring in depth what this means for people in my master’s thesis, “Artificially Intelligent Cities”. The thesis was nominated for the RIBA Dissertation Medals, and I will continue this research at ENSA Versailles.

How architecture chooses to respond is a question we must answer anew every day. A new way of thinking is needed—an evolution of our design culture. I seek new approaches at the intersection of architecture, technology and experimental research. In “Abyssora”, I worked with a collaborator to explore possibilities for reusing container ships as architectural elements at the end of their service life. Creating a digital inventory allowed us to make existing ship components and their properties the starting point of the design. The project received the “Grand Prix pour la Mer” from the Fondation Jacques Rougerie and was published in two magazines. We are now developing it further with a focus on its feasibility.

A world of connectivity, speed and dislocation also gives rise to new ways of living. Engaging with these changes gives us the opportunity to help shape them. In “Voluntary Exile” and “Synthetic Exodus”, I explore my own ideas through narratives and spatial designs. These projects show an architecture shaped by my own experiences. They ask how communal living can generate the unexpected and how a nomadic life can coexist with a permanent home. So the circle closes, returning to reality—not the one I encounter, but the one I wish for and want to help shape.

Josua Hefti
